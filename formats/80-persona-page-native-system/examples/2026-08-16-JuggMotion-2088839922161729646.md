## Full text

> growth is not a slope you climb by doing more of the same thing harder. it's a staircase, and every step has a different constraint sitting on it.
> 
> a thousand-dollar day has a different problem than a three-thousand-dollar day. thirty thousand a day is a different animal than both.
> 
> you need to be running a constant feedback loop of point-of-constraint analysis: at any moment, ONE thing is capping your growth. find it, fix it, and a new constraint shows up at the next level. that's literally what scaling is.
> 
> the brand in this case study was doing a few hundred a day when I came on. the first constraint wasn't ads and it wasn't creative (it almost never is down there). it was the funnel leaking money after the click. we'll get to that.
> 
> your only job at every level: name your current constraint out loud before you spend another dollar. if you can't name it, you're not scaling — you're gambling.
> 
> product selection math
> 
> none of it matters if the product can't carry the weight. three criteria, all mandatory:
> 
> - high TAM — a market big enough that you can be wrong about half your targeting and still eat
> - an emotional pain point — people don't move fast for mild preferences. they move fast for pain
> - multiple avatars — the same product has to make sense to completely different people, because that's how you multiply winners later (hold that thought for the whitelist section)
> 
> then the margin math. I want a 5x markup now — when I started I'd take a 3x or a 4x, but margin is oxygen, and thin margin suffocates you at the exact moment things start working.
> 
> on the customer side: 3:1 LTV to CAC minimum. with repeat purchases you can push it to 9-12:1. this brand was a supplement — the product runs out every 30 days, so the reorder IS the business model. break-even on the front end with a fat back end beats front-end profit with no repeat purchase every single time.
> 
> the cost hierarchy
> 
> stop reading your ad account in ratios. ROAS tells you something is wrong. dollars tell you where.
> 
> price every step of the funnel as its own cost:
> 
> - CPV — what a view costs you
> - CPC — what an “unique outbound” click costs, NOT ANY click
> - CPLP — what a landing page view costs you
> - CPATC — what an add to cart costs you
> - CPIC - what an initiated checkout costs you
> - CPP — what a purchase costs you
> 
> read it left to right and the leak exposes itself. views cheap but clicks expensive? CAN BE THAT the ad isn't earning the click — creative problem, take into account that some audiences cost more & meta does give you more expensive CPMs with new ad accounts. clicks cheap but add to carts expensive? page problem. add to carts cheap but purchases expensive? checkout problem — trust, shipping shock, payment options.
> 
> and burn this number in: 80% of visitors never get past the add-to-cart button. most of your funnel dies in the last two steps, while most operators pour all their energy into the first two.
> 
> that was one of our brand's first constraint. everyone around it wanted to talk about new angles. the leak was at checkout. we fixed the back of the funnel first, THEN scaled spend into it. that's the discipline: fix the leak before you pay to pour more water in the bucket.
> 
> once the funnel holds water, creative volume becomes the constraint. we ran two engines side by side and they did different jobs:
> 
> image ads are the machine gun. every image editor put out 3-5 concepts a day. cheap to make, fast to read, and you can spray them across every angle you're curious about. volume is the whole point.
> 
> video ads are the sniper. every video editor did 5 concepts a week. slower, pricier, aimed carefully — homerun swings on angles the machine gun already proved.
> 
> around those two: rips (study what's already spending in your market before you ideate from zero), product seeding to creators for raw footage, and AI UGC to pad the volume even further.
> 
> say you pay an editor $150 a week for 5 ads. that's $30 an ad. at a 10% hit rate, a winning ad costs you $300, and at a 1% hit rate that same winner costs you $3,000.
> 
> hit rate is a real cost lever in creative, not JUST editor pay. it improves with better research, tighter briefs, and iterating on proven concepts instead of guessing from a blank page & brain dumping into a claude chat. put your energy there.
> 
> whitelist persona pages
> 
> cheapest scaling move in the whole system i’ve seen is just to change who's saying it.
> 
> take a winning creative and run the exact same thing from a different persona page — a personal page, an authority page, a themed page. same angle, same footage, different face on the account delivering it.
> 
> each persona reaches a pocket of the audience the original never touched, because people buy from people who look like their world. one winner quietly becomes three or four. and this is why multiple avatars was a product criterion — you built for different people on day one, now you deliver from different people.
> 
> here is the order in which i hire for new stores, now most of these can be replaced with claude code so idk but yeah.
> 
> 1. customer support VA — either you do it yourself and lose money for focusing on low ROI tasks, or you don’t do it at all and lose money because you’re ignoring customers and getting fucked by chargebacks
> 
> 2. image editors — feed the machine gun & quickly pump out more tests
> 
> 3. exec VA — takes the bit higher level tasks off (job posts, contracts, paying, etc.)
> 
> 4. video editor — the sniper comes after volume exists, not before.
> 
> 5. creative strategist — ideally you’re training junior editors up to be strategist, then go out adn hire sum killer senior strategist
> 
> i have a decent following so hiring isn’t too difficult, if you don’t just go on upwork, linkedin, etc. & pay that extra amount to push your posts it. It is worth it.
> 
> now, product sourcing.
> 
> around this time last year we were sourcing supplements through a dropshipping middleman at $11 all-in with no idea whether it worked or not tbh. now I go direct factory where we ACTUALLY know the active formula %, get the COA, third party tests, etc. and pay at max $3/unit + 6 to ship. Worse than the margin hit was the speed: every new flavor, every packaging change, moved at the middleman's pace instead of ours.
> 
> so I got on a plane and went direct. visited the factory, verified the documents, built the relationship face to face and now we have:
> 
> - 500 MOQ with production in 2-5 days — meanwhile a US manufacturer quoted me 80,000 MOQ + a fucking month for a product I hadn't even launched yet
> - payment terms: started at 14-day net, negotiated toward 20-day, then ideally we can get to 90 by end of year. I know you’re reading this tweet by the way. Let me get 90 days bro. Please.
> 
> If you guys watched that Gulfam interview dude is a killer, and was talking about getting 180-day net terms with his supplier. Nigga. Thats half a year. Insane.
> 
> your supply chain is either a margin machine and a cash-flow weapon, or it's a tax with a countdown attached. there's no neutral. Especially as you’re looking to scale, things like cash conversion cycles heavily are based off how often you have to pay your balances & how fast you get paid money owed.
> 
> if you're starting today, in order i’d recommend:
> 
> 1. pick the product with the three criteria — high TAM, emotional pain, multiple avatars. want a 5x markup ideally & a path to have customers purchase repeatedly (more product launches, mrr plays, etc.)
> 
> 2. use a spytool to identify winning angles, launch off of these then find the constraint keeping you from scaling it
> 
> 3. start the machine gun. image ads w/pages built specifically for them
> 
> 4. name your point of constraint and focus ONLY that. fixing things that aren't the constraint feels productive and does nothing.
> 
> 5. go back to step 4
> 
> everything above runs on one loop: observation → hypothesis → test → conclusion. observe the data, hypothesis why, test the hypothesis, keep the answer — and the answer becomes your next observation. every section in this article is just that loop pointed at a different part of the machine.
> 
> and the loop cuts both ways. read and minimize your risks like you do your ads.
