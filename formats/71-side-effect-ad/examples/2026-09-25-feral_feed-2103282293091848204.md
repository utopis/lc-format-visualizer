## Full text

> Ads that would get a modern brand cancelled built Abercrombie’s most valuable era.
> 
> Mike Jeffries didn’t hide the strategy. 2006, Salon interview: the retailer didn’t carry XL or XXL sizes for women because it didn’t want people who weren’t thin and beautiful wearing its clothes. His own words: “we want to market to cool, good-looking people. We don’t market to anyone other than that.”
> 
> The tennis ad, the shirtless-triangle ad, all of it was that philosophy made visual. Exclusion wasn’t a side effect of the brand. It was the product.
> 
> Then the internet caught up to him. Backlash, lawsuits, a 2022 Netflix documentary. Sales cratered. By 2016 Abercrombie was ranked America’s most hated retailer.
> 
> Fran Horowitz took over in 2017 and did the opposite of everything Jeffries built. Inclusive sizing. No more exclusionary casting. No more “cool kids only.”
> 
> In 2023 that version of Abercrombie was the best-performing stock in the entire S&P 1500. It beat Nvidia.
> 
> Same logo. Two completely different businesses. One sold belonging through rejection. The other sold it through inclusion, and made more money doing it.
> 
> The nostalgia in these ads isn’t for the clothes. It’s for a marketing era that couldn’t survive its own business model.
