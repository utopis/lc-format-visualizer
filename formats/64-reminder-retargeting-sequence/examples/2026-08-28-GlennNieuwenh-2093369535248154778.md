## Full text

> If I took over a struggling store tomorrow...
> 
> I wouldn't open the ads manager on day one:
> 
> I'd start with the numbers.
> 
> First, a real P&L.
> 
> - Cost of goods and shipping
> - Processing fees on every order
> - Refunds, and chargeback fees including the ones they won
> - The full software stack
> - Staff and customer service
> - Currency conversion and transfer costs
> 
> Then work out what a single order actually nets.
> 
> Plenty of stores that look like they have a traffic problem are just running products that were never going to be profitable at that price.
> 
> No amount of creative fixes that.
> 
> Second, the cash position.
> 
> What's held in payment reserves, what's locked up in inventory, and what's owed to the supplier and when.
> 
> That decides what moves are even available.
> 
> A store with $25k tied up in reserves and stock has completely different options to one sitting on cash, even if the revenue line looks identical.
> 
> Third, the offer.
> 
> This is the cheapest thing to fix and it lifts every ad running behind it.
> 
> Are there bonuses with a stated value, or just a price sitting there on its own.
> 
> Is there a bundle that raises order value.
> 
> Does the guarantee actually remove the risk.
> 
> How does the whole thing compare to what competitors are stacking.
> 
> A weak offer caps everything downstream, and almost nobody touches it because creative feels more productive.
> 
> Fourth, where the traffic lands.
> 
> If cold traffic is going straight to a product page, that's usually the ceiling.
> 
> You're asking a stranger to buy within seconds of meeting the brand.
> 
> I'd also click their own ads and walk the whole path, checking whether the message survives from ad to page to checkout.
> 
> Fifth, the creative.
> 
> Not how many ads they've run.
> 
> How different those ads actually are from each other.
> 
> 30 versions of the same angle counts as roughly 1 test, and it explains why nothing ever seems to work despite all the volume.
> 
> Sixth, what happens after someone buys.
> 
> Whether there's a welcome flow, an abandoned cart sequence, anything pushing a second order.
> 
> If the store is only profitable on the first purchase, the whole business is capped at whatever a cold stranger spends on day one.
> 
> The product comes last, and that surprises people.
> 
> Almost every time I've looked at a store that was stuck, the product was fine.
> 
> The pricing, the offer, the page, or the funnel was losing the sale before the product ever got a fair test.
> 
> So you fix the leaks in order of what costs the most money.
> 
> One change at a time, so you can actually read what moved.
> 
> The instinct when something's broken is to change everything at once, and that guarantees you learn nothing from any of it.
